    About the Deep Time Atlas

    Each icon marks fossils found at that spot, plotted on today’s continents. By default an icon’s size compares it with the richest spot for its kind of animal or plant (dinosaurs with dinosaurs, fish with fish) at that moment, so the dominant animals of every age stand out, whether that age has a rich fossil record (the Late Cretaceous) or a thin one (the Middle Jurassic). Switch “Icon size” to “Compared with each group’s peak” to size each group against its own best moment instead, which shows when a group was at its height but makes ages with fewer fossils look empty.

    Coloured patches show which group dominates each region. “Most fossils nearby” colours each area by the group with the most fossil records within a few hundred kilometres. “Each group’s heartland” scales every group against its own densest region first, so heavily collected groups (turtles, theropod teeth) do not swamp rarer ones. Patches only show where fossils of that age were found, so gaps are gaps in the rock record as much as in life.

    Community names follow palaeontological practice. A fauna is named after its characteristic genus, as in Lehman’s Triceratops and Alamosaurus faunas of the latest Cretaceous, the Karoo’s Lystrosaurus Assemblage Zone, or Ziegler’s Pentamerus community of Silurian seas. Here the characteristic genus is the one that is both common in the region and concentrated there rather than spread worldwide; a second genus is added only when it is nearly as characteristic and belongs to a different group. Land and sea assemblages are mapped and named separately, using the depositional environment recorded for each fossil (or the animal’s way of life where none is recorded). Marine assemblages dominated by shelly invertebrates are called communities. The second line gives the rock formation that supplies most of the fossils and the commonest environment. Form-genera of isolated teeth, footprints, eggs and dispersed spores and pollen stay on the map but are not used as names. Plant assemblages are called floras, as in the classic Glossopteris flora of Gondwana, and microfossil and microbial assemblages are called assemblages.

    Climate. Global mean surface temperature and CO₂ from 485 Ma to today come from PhanDA, a data-assimilation reconstruction that combines 150,000 fossil and chemical temperature measurements with climate-model runs (Judd et al. 2024, Science); the readout gives the median and the 5–95% range. Air temperature by latitude is PhanDA’s reconstructed gradient for each stage; sea-surface temperature by latitude is PhanDA’s gradient for the closest “climate state” (coldhouse to hothouse). Oxygen from 550 Ma, and CO₂ before 485 Ma, come from the GEOCARBSULFOR carbon–sulfur cycle model (Krause et al. 2018; 2022 Monte Carlo runs); its very high Permian oxygen (about 35%) is at the top of published estimates, and charcoal records suggest closer to 25–30%. Ice caps are drawn poleward of the latitude where mean annual air temperature falls below −10 °C, plus dated glaciations and “snowball Earth” events before the Phanerozoic. Temperature belts and ice are by latitude, because the reconstructions are by latitude; with today’s continent positions they show the climate zones of the time, not where each landmass then sat. Before 485 Ma no temperature is shown, and Precambrian oxygen and CO₂ are rough ranges only (after Catling & Zahnle 2020 and Lyons et al. 2014), shown as bands.

    Dinosaur groups. Dinosaurs are grouped by clade using the Paleobiology Database’s classification: diplodocoids; brachiosaurs, camarasaurs and titanosaurs (macronarians); early sauropodomorphs; stegosaurs; ankylosaurs; ceratosaurs and abelisaurs; allosaurs, megalosaurs and spinosaurs; tyrannosaurs; raptors (dromaeosaurs and troodontids); ostrich mimics, oviraptors and other feathered theropods; ceratopsians; pachycephalosaurs; hadrosaurs; and iguanodonts with other small ornithischians. Teeth, eggs and footprints are kept and go to the most specific group their name allows: a dromaeosaur tooth to the raptors, a hadrosaur egg to the hadrosaurs. Fossils that can only be placed as “theropod”, such as the common Triassic and Jurassic footprints Grallator and Eubrontes, go to “Other theropods & theropod tracks”, and those that cannot be placed at all to “Earliest & unplaced dinosaurs”.

    Showing one group. Tapping a group’s name in the panel, or finding a genus, shows that group alone: its icons, patches and community names, and nothing else. Tap the name again, or close the genus card, to bring the other groups back.

    Search. Type a genus (or a group) in the search box, or press “/”. The map jumps to the genus’s peak in the fossil record, the 5-million-year window with the most records, and zooms to its heartland, the densest cluster of finds at that time. Rings mark its fossils: solid rings are in the current time window, faint dots are from other times. Its record is drawn on the timeline. A link such as #Dunkleosteus on the page address opens straight to that genus.

    Daggers (†) on the timeline mark mass extinctions; the five largest are drawn bold. Near each one the map shows its probable cause, such as the Chicxulub impact site or the Siberian Traps (plotted at present-day positions). Species-loss figures for the Big Five are estimates from Barnosky et al. (2011).

    The timeline reaches back 4,000 million years. The Precambrian is compressed: 4,000–1,000 Ma and 1,000–541 Ma each take a tenth of the slider, marked by dashed lines. The Precambrian record is thin and patchy, so the time window widens there, to 20 million years for the Neoproterozoic and 50 million years before that, and playback speeds up. Precambrian stromatolites come from stromatolite-bearing rock units in Macrostrat (mostly North America), plus the PaleoReefs database; other Precambrian fossils come from the Paleobiology Database. Continents stay in their present positions throughout, so the oldest records sit far from where they formed.

    In the Phanerozoic the time window is 5 million years wide. A fossil dated only loosely (for example “Late Cretaceous”, 34 million years) is spread across its whole range, so it counts for less at any one moment than a precisely dated find.

    Climate data: PhanDA (Apache 2.0) and GEOCARBSULFOR outputs, both from their authors’ public GitHub repositories. Fossil occurrences come from the Paleobiology Database (CC BY 4.0), via public exports in three open-source projects: petra (classified dinosaurs and birds), dinotime (reptiles) and the Heim lab’s PBDB_Scripts (about 650,000 animal occurrences across the last 540 million years). These are snapshots, so the newest finds are missing. Plants, algae, fungi, cyanobacteria and plankton (foraminifera, coccolithophores, diatoms, radiolarians, dinoflagellates, acritarchs) were downloaded from the Paleobiology Database on 4 November 2026. Stromatolites and other microbial reefs come from the PaleoReefs database (Kiessling and colleagues), as packaged in the palaeoverse R package. Stromatolites were most widespread before the Cambrian, which is earlier than this map goes. Plants and microbes are switched off by default; tick them in the panel. Dinosaur names link to their pages on dinosaurpictures.org, which is built on the same database.
