About the Deep Time Atlas

Each icon marks fossils found at that spot, plotted on today’s continents. Drag the timeline and the icons grow where a group is common in rocks of that age and shrink as it becomes rare. Sizes are relative to each group’s own peak, so a rare animal like Velociraptor still shows at full size where it is best known.

Coloured patches show which group dominates each region. “Most fossils nearby” colours each area by the group with the most fossil records within a few hundred kilometres. “Each group’s heartland” scales every group against its own densest region first, so heavily collected groups (turtles, theropod teeth) do not swamp rarer ones. Patches only show where fossils of that age were found, so gaps are gaps in the rock record as much as in life.

Community names follow palaeontological practice. A fauna is named after its characteristic genus, as in Lehman’s Triceratops and Alamosaurus faunas of the latest Cretaceous, the Karoo’s Lystrosaurus Assemblage Zone, or Ziegler’s Pentamerus community of Silurian seas. Here the characteristic genus is the one that is both common in the region and concentrated there rather than spread worldwide; a second genus is added only when it is nearly as characteristic and belongs to a different group. Land and sea assemblages are mapped and named separately, using the depositional environment recorded for each fossil (or the animal’s way of life where none is recorded). Marine assemblages dominated by shelly invertebrates are called communities. The second line gives the rock formation that supplies most of the fossils and the commonest environment. Tooth and footprint form-genera are not used as names.

Daggers (†) on the timeline mark mass extinctions; the five largest are drawn bold. Near each one the map shows its probable cause, such as the Chicxulub impact site or the Siberian Traps (plotted at present-day positions). Species-loss figures for the Big Five are estimates from Barnosky et al. (2011).

The time window is 5 million years wide. A fossil dated only loosely (for example “Late Cretaceous”, 34 million years) is spread across its whole range, so it counts for less at any one moment than a precisely dated find.

Fossil occurrences come from the Paleobiology Database (CC BY 4.0), via public exports in three open-source projects: petra (classified dinosaurs and birds), dinotime (reptiles) and the Heim lab’s PBDB_Scripts (about 650,000 animal occurrences across the last 540 million years). These are snapshots, so the newest finds are missing. Dinosaur names link to their pages on dinosaurpictures.org, which is built on the same database.
